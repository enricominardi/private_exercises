Part II - A/B Test¶

1. For now, consider you need to make the decision just based on all the data provided. If you want to assume that the old page is better unless the new page proves to be definitely better at a Type I error rate of 5%, what should your null and alternative hypotheses be? You can state your hypothesis in terms of words or in terms of $p_{old}$ and $p_{new}$, which are the converted rates for the old and new pages.

$p_{old}$ := conversion rate for the old page

$p_{new}$ := conversion rate for the new page

We can assocate a parameter to each of the over-mentioned statistics. Let us call them $\pi_{old}$ and $\pi_{new}$, so that:

$H_o$: $\pi_{new} \leq \pi_{old}$ $\rightarrow$ $\pi_{new} - \pi_{old} = \pi_{\Delta} \stackrel{!}{\leq} 0 $

$H_1$: $\pi_{new} > \pi_{old}$ $\rightarrow$ $\pi_{new} - \pi_{old} = \pi_{\Delta} \stackrel{!}{>} 0$

$\alpha$ = 0,05

h. Simulate 10,000 $p_{new}$ - $p_{old}$ values using this same process similarly to the one you calculated in parts a. through g. above. Store all 10,000 values in a numpy array called p_diffs.

Note: For now we will be talking about the distribution of a parameter, we will call $\pi_{\Delta}$ the parameter associated with our statistic $p_{diff}$, and $\Pi_{\Delta}$ its sampling distribution.

In [32]:
p_diffs = []

for _ in range(10000):
    #we create two samples with size equal to n_new and n_old
    sample_old = df2.sample(n_old, replace=True)
    sample_new = df2.sample(n_new, replace=True)
    # and calculate p_new, p_old for each sample
    pi_old = sample_old.query('converted == 1').user_id.count()/sample_old.shape[0]
    pi_new = sample_new.query('converted == 1').user_id.count()/sample_new.shape[0]
    # we save the difference in a list
    p_diffs.append(pi_new - pi_old)

e. What is the p-value associated with ab_page? Why does it differ from the value you found in Part II?

Hint: What are the null and alternative hypotheses associated with your regression model, and how do they compare to the null and alternative hypotheses in the Part II?

Answer

The p-value associated with the ab_page coefficient (let us call the coefficient $\beta_{ab\_page}$) is 0.190.

Concerning the null hypothses, we have here two pairs of null an alternative hypotheses - one for the intercept and one for ab_page

For instance, the null and the alternative hypotheses for $\beta_{ab\_page}$ are:

$H_0$: $\beta_{ab\_page} \stackrel{!}{=} \beta_{ab\_page, o} := 0$ $\rightarrow$ $\beta_{ab\_page} - \beta_{ab\_page, o} \stackrel{!}{=} 0$

$H_1$: $\beta_{ab\_page} \neq \beta_{ab\_page, o} := 0$ $\rightarrow$ $\beta_{ab\_page} - \beta_{ab\_page, o} \neq 0$

where $\beta_{ab\_page, o}$ is the value of $\beta_{ab\_page}$ under the null.

The p-value associated with $\beta_{ab\_page}$ is the probability of more extreme z-values in the tails (for this is a two-sided test) of the distribution of $\beta_{ab\_page} - \beta_{ab\_page, o}$ under the null, with respect of the z statistic computed.

Therefore, we can reject $H_0$, if the p-value is smaller than our $\alpha$.

Model 1 - Results

According to this Logit model, $\beta_{ab\_page}$ is indeed not significative. Its p-value is greater than any $\alpha$ < 0.19.

Hence, we cannot reject the null - meaning, there is no evidence of an effect of the new page on the probability of conversion.

Moreover and accordingly, the pseudo-$R^2$ is equal to zero - $i.e.$ the restrictions imposed on the coefficients (in this case, same $H_o$ as above, plus a similar $H_o$ for the intercept) do not 'bite' (meaning, restricted model and unrestricted model perform similarily).

However, we will not focus on the pseudo-$R^2$ in what follows, for it has no 'straightforward' interpretation, due to the nonlinearity of the model.

Compared to the conclusions in Part II, 2.m, the results are (lato sensu) concordant. Of course, we cannot expect exactly the same numerical values, for ($e.g.$):

  1. the object of interest is not the same. We have specified $H_0$ in terms of conversion rates (Part II), rather than coefficients of a binary variable (Part III);
  2. the null for $\beta_{ab\_page}$ is expressed in such a way that a two-sided test is needed, while the test was one-sided in Part II;
  3. the method used is not the same (we used bootstrapping, where here we use regression on an explicit logistic model, optimized numerically by ML;
  4. Even though $\pi_{\Delta}$ and $\beta_{ab\_page}$ should be both Normally distributed, it is highly unlikely that their distributions share the same moments.

Conclusions¶

We have investigated the relation between the page being displayed to a user and the frequency of conversion in our data set 'ab_data.csv'.

In Part I, we have constructed a statstic of the probability of conversion, given the page visited. We have not found sufficient evidence to claim that the new page leads to more conversions. Conversely, we observed a slightly higher conversion frequency within the control group (12.04%; old page) than in the experiment group (11.88%, new page).

In Part II, we have performed an A/B Test for the difference in conversion rates between pages against a sampling distribution of the same difference, which has been obtained by bootstrapping our data set under the null of no positive 'new-page' effect.

In the first place, we have validated the consistency of the so-obtained sampling distribution mean to our expected value under the null hypothesis, as specified in Part II, 1.

In the second place, we have compared our statistic $p_{diff}$ with the reference parameter $\pi_{\Delta}$. We have found that $p_{diff}$ is consistent with the null hypotesis. Therefore, there is no evidence in favour of claiming that the new page leads to a higher conversion rate.

We have validated this result by performing a 'Proportions z-Test'. This test provied concordant results to the ones mentioned above.

In Part III, we applied regression techniques on explicit Logit models.

On a first model, we have assessed the effect of a page on the probability of conversion. Although not signficant, the estimation of $\beta_{ab\_page}$ was negative. Both, the non-significance of $\beta_{ab\_page}$ and the negativity of its estimated value, are concorant with the results in Part II. We then suggested to add the time stamp to the model, in oder to control for possible aversion (of old users) to the new page. If the time stamp turns out to be significative, it will be a good idea to run the experiment longer.

A second model included data on user residency. We have assessed the effect of three countries of origin on the probability of conversion. The estimate of $\beta_{ab\_page}$ was still non significative and negative. However, the model highlighted a negative relation between the dependent variable and the country for users located in Canada, whereas this relation was not significative for users from the UK and from the US.

We have then focused on the interaction between page and country. We assessed these interactions in a third model and found out that the cross effect of new page and country was significant for Canada, but not for UK and USA.

Finally, we computed a last model (fourth), by considering just the interaction between the new page and residency in Canada. The interaction coefficient for the new page and Canada was negaive and significant at 5%. This model provides evidence that the probability of converting is 1.08 times lower for a user living in Canada and viewing the new page, $caeteris$ $paribus$.

Generally, the results obtained in Part I, Part II and Part III are concordant on two claims.

  1. There is no evidence in favour of claiming that the new page leads to a higher probability of conversion;

  2. Conversely, there is evidence of a negative effect of the new page on the probability of conversion. We have seen now that this can be reconducted mainly to users located in Canada.

Consequently, we suggest to run the experiment longer and assess the effect of the time stamps on the probability of conversion. If this is not possible, the evidence gathered until now suggests to keep the old page.
